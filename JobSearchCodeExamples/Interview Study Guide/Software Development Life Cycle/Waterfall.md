# Waterfall Model Development Methodology
The Waterfall model is a linear and sequential approach to software
development. It is one of the earliest formal software development
methodologies and is characterized by completing major phases of the
project in sequence.

The model assumes that requirements can be defined relatively early
and that significant changes will be uncommon or can be managed 
through a formal change process.

## Process Steps
1. **Requirements Analysis**: Gather, analyze, and document project
   requirements.

2. **System Design**: Create a detailed design based on the approved
   requirements.

3. **Implementation**: Develop the software according to the approved
   design.

4. **Testing**: Verify that the software satisfies the documented
   requirements and identify defects.

5. **Deployment**: Release the completed software to the production
   environment.

6. **Maintenance**: Correct defects and make approved changes to the
   deployed software.

The defining characteristic of Waterfall is that these phases are
performed primarily in sequence. The project generally does not begin
full implementation until requirements and design have been completed.

## Characteristics
- Requirements are defined early.
- Each phase produces documented deliverables.
- Progress generally follows a sequential process.
- Changes made later in the project can be expensive.
- Testing occurs primarily after implementation.
- Documentation is typically extensive.
- Project progress can be measured against predefined milestones.

## Advantages
- Clear project structure and milestones.
- Well-defined requirements and deliverables.
- Extensive documentation.
- Easier to estimate schedules and costs when requirements are 
  stable.
- Can work well in environments requiring formal approval processes.

## Disadvantages
- Difficult and expensive to accommodate changing requirements.
- Problems in requirements or design may not be discovered until 
  later.
- Working software is generally not available until relatively late
  in the project.
- Testing occurs after most implementation has been completed.
- Customers may have limited opportunities to provide feedback 
  during development.

## When Waterfall Can Be Appropriate
Waterfall can be appropriate when:

- Requirements are well understood.
- Requirements are unlikely to change significantly.
- The technology and implementation approach are well understood.
- Formal documentation and approval are required.
- The project has regulatory or contractual requirements that favor
  predefined specifications and milestones.

## Waterfall vs. Iterative Development
The major difference between Waterfall and iterative methodologies is
when feedback and change are incorporated.

In Waterfall, requirements and design are established early and the
project progresses through defined phases.

In iterative development, the system is developed in smaller 
increments, allowing requirements, design, implementation, and 
testing to be revisited as the project progresses.

## Common Interview Questions

**What is the Waterfall development model?**

**What are the advantages and disadvantages of Waterfall?**

**When would you choose Waterfall instead of an Agile methodology?**

**Why can changing requirements be expensive in a Waterfall project?**

**How does Waterfall differ from iterative development?**

**Can testing occur before implementation is complete in Waterfall?**

Yes. Individual testing activities can occur throughout development,
but traditional Waterfall generally places the primary system testing
phase after implementation.

## My Experience
When I started at Cougar Mountain Software, the team was developing
software using a Waterfall approach. Requirements were gathered and
documented before coding began. The design was then completed, the
feature was implemented, and the completed feature was tested.

This process worked well for well-defined features, but when 
requirements changed or were not well-defined, it caused problems. 
Changes could require revisiting requirements and design decisions 
that had already been completed.

The team eventually moved toward a more iterative approach, which
allowed for more flexibility and feedback during development. This 
made it easier to adapt to changing requirements and identify 
problems earlier in the development process.
